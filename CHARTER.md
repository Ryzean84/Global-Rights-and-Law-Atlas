# Project Charter

## Mission
The Global Rights and Law Atlas is an open-source civic knowledge project that helps people discover and understand laws, founding documents, rights protections, legal changes, and public-interest reporting relevant to the places where they live, travel, work, publish, and build technology.

> **We the People, not We the Corporation.**

## What the Atlas is
- A structured index of primary legal sources and their histories.
- A geographic discovery layer connecting places to the jurisdictions that govern them.
- A multilingual reference system preserving original texts and separately recording translations.
- A public contribution process for source discovery, verification, corrections, journalism references, and community perspectives.
- An open-source method that independent groups can clone, operate, mirror, and adapt.

## What it is not
- A law firm, legal adviser, court, regulator, or substitute for qualified local counsel.
- A system that declares one country's laws superior to another's.
- A tool for evading law or manufacturing legal legitimacy.
- A truth-ranking engine or automated judge of public submissions.
- A centralized owner of community knowledge or a promise that every distributed copy can be recalled.

## Core principles
1. People-centered access, including low-bandwidth and multilingual access.
2. Jurisdictional precision across countries, states, provinces, municipalities, tribal nations, territories, and supranational systems.
3. Primary sources first: official gazettes, legislatures, courts, treaty depositaries, and government archives.
4. Provenance: issuer, origin, publication date, effective date, and verification date.
5. Historical accuracy: preserve versions, amendments, effective dates, repeals, stays, and superseding instruments.
6. Plural voices: keep law, interpretation, journalism, testimony, and reform proposals distinct.
7. Privacy and safety for vulnerable contributors and sources.
8. Open participation through transparent pull requests, review, and forks.
9. Accessibility: Unicode, right-to-left scripts, language tags, screen readers, and low-resource devices.
10. Learning in public: document errors and corrections without shaming contributors.

## Geographic gateway model
International airports are early discovery anchors because they connect people to countries and regions. They are not legal boundaries. Each airport anchor must link to explicitly identified jurisdictions, and each jurisdiction must be independently sourced. Expansion can include ports, border crossings, cities, towns, counties, states, provinces, tribal nations, islands, courts, universities, libraries, and local archives.

## Public participation and reform
The Atlas may host clearly labeled observations, journalism references, questions, and reform proposals. It does not decide whether a law should change. It helps people locate the text, understand its status, compare documented approaches, and find lawful public participation channels. Submissions must not encourage violence, harassment, doxxing, or unlawful evasion.

## Integrity and distributed preservation
Git history, checksums, signatures, append-only event logs, Merkle trees, and independent mirrors may make records tamper-evident. They do not establish truth, legal validity, completeness, or fairness. The project does not require cryptocurrency or a public blockchain. Any future distributed ledger needs a documented threat model, privacy review, governance model, and clear advantage over simpler signed and replicated records.

## Governance
Maintainers manage repository operations; they do not become authorities over the laws or communities represented. Review decisions should be documented and open to challenge and forks. A future governance model should include regional and language maintainers, legal-source reviewers, journalists, accessibility contributors, and community representatives.

## Legal and safety notice
The Atlas is informational, not legal advice. Laws change, translations may be unofficial, and outcomes depend on facts and local interpretation. Consult authoritative local sources and qualified professionals for consequential decisions. Do not submit privileged communications, private source identities, credentials, sensitive personal data, or material that could expose a person to harm.
